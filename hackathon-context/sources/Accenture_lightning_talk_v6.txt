KEY MESSAGE: A trustworthy AI system has to be proven fit before it goes live, and proven to stay fit while it runs. One without the other is not enough.

TALKING POINTS:
- Walk the six stages left to right. The first four sit before production: register the use case and owner, build it with versioned data, models, agents and prompts, test it against metrics and thresholds, and pass an approval gate with a named approver.
- The last two sit in production: monitor live, with guardrails, alerts and remediation, and re-assess on material change or retire with the records kept.
- Point to the loop. Drift, incidents and regulatory change send a production system back to testing. This is a cycle, not a line.
- Why both phases? Three properties of AI break the assumptions of classic software controls:
  1. Behaviour is probabilistic. A model has an error rate as a property of the system. "Correct" is a distribution, not a value.
  2. Performance decays without any change. Nobody deployed anything; the world moved. That is why monitoring has to be treated as a control, not just an operational one.
  3. Humans over-trust the output. A human in the loop reliably degrades into confirmation when the system is right most of the time. You can see it in the data: agreement rates climbing, override counts falling.
- The next two slides show the same journey in more detail: first what the organization sets up once, then what every AI system goes through.

Probabilistic behavior: A model has an error rate, not a bug. “Correct” is a distribution, so pass / fail against a specification has nothing to grip.

Performance decay:  The world moves. Accuracy can degrade with no deployment and no change ticket, so monitoring is a control.

Humans over trust With a system that is usually right, human review drifts into confirmation. Oversight itself has to be measured

KEY MESSAGE: Before any individual AI system can be compliant, the organization has to set up the machinery once: governance, acceptable risk, policies and controls, assessments and an inventory.

TALKING POINTS:
- 01 Take self assessments: maturity to uptake AI, GenAI and RAI; readiness for legislation such as the EU AI Act and NIST; benchmark against competitors.
- 02 Set up the governance body: CoE members and standards, meeting frequency, reviewers, escalation pathways, and which organizations must comply by geography.
- 03 Define risk, policies and controls: select applicable legislation, define acceptable risks, define RAI policies and controls, enable practitioners, set review frequency. Note "define acceptable risks" comes before "define controls". We return to that ordering in Part 3.
- 04 Select AI systems' assessments: which risk scoring and qualitative assessments apply, the workflow to answer them, and the approval process for experimentation and pre-production.
- 05 Create the AI systems' inventory: every system in pre-production and production, with metadata.
- 06 Perform AI system : contact owners, score risk, answer assessments, test before production, monitor in production.
- The strip at the bottom: steps 1 to 3 are GRC set-up; steps 4 to 6 apply it to each AI system.

KEY MESSAGE: Every AI system goes through five stages. Four happen before production and prove it is fit; the fifth runs for as long as the system is live.

TALKING POINTS:
- 01 Provide metadata: use case, geography, AI problems solved, models, agents and test datasets. Everything later depends on this record.
- 02 Answer risk assessments: questionnaires determine a risk level of low, medium or high. That tier decides how much scrutiny follows.
- 03 Answer qualitative assessments: use-case specific information that a risk score cannot capture.
- 04 Pre-production testing: choose metrics and thresholds, compute them on test data, evaluate pass or fail, remediate and re-run until compliant, then send the report for approval.
- 05 Continuous monitoring: guardrails, threshold alerts, real-time remediation, dashboards, incident management and periodic reporting.
- Notice the words that keep appearing: thresholds, pass / fail, remediation, report, approval. Those are GRC words. To make this workflow defensible we need that vocabulary.

KEY MESSAGE: Three things to remember.

TALKING POINTS:
- One: trust is proven twice, before production and continuously in production.
- Two: a control is something you do that somebody else can check, and what it produces must be evidence a stranger can verify, not just a log.
- Three: decide the risk you can accept first and derive the controls from it; then watch that risk live, because for AI it moves.
- Return to the opening idea: a trustworthy AI system is one whose safeguards can be checked by somebody who does not trust you.
